Abstract

The initial impetus for composing this edited volume came from the continued prevalence of problematic practices in the extraction of natural resources, both minerals and energy resources, worldwide. This has inspired our research conducted within the scientific project MINIPART – Improving Participation in Spatial Planning of Mining Regions, supported by the Science Fund of the Republic of Serbia, #7598. Despite major advances in international and national legislations, policy frameworks, and various voluntary guidelines intended to improve environmental governance and social responsibility in mining practice, local communities in extraction regions still bear the social, environmental, economic, and cultural burdens of extraction processes, while the majority of benefits remain concentrated within national governments and mining corporations. This imbalance continues to generate conflicts, mistrust, and long-term socio-spatial inequalities, particularly in regions heavily dependent on extractive industries. In the international literature and practice, this has been recognised as the “natural resource curse” or the “paradox of plenty”, where regions rich in resources (especially in oil, gas, or minerals) generally experience slower economic growth, weaker democracy, more severe environmental degradation and overall worse development outcomes than the resource-poor countries, while the macro-level financial windfalls concentrate with national and regional authorities and multinational corporations.
